# One Week, One Book
## Episode 1: *Death and the Gardener*, by Georgi Gospodinov

“My father was a gardener. Now he is a garden.”

I want to begin with that sentence because, for me, it holds the heart of this book. It is simple, but it changes as you sit with it. First, it tells us what the father did. Then it turns that fact into an image: the father, the garden, and the way a person can remain in someone else’s memory.

Welcome to *One Week, One Book*, a podcast about choosing a book and taking the time to ask why it might be worth reading. This week, I’m talking about *Death and the Gardener*, by the Bulgarian writer Georgi Gospodinov.

Before I say more, a small note about the subject. This book deals with illness, death and grief. I’m recommending it for its literary qualities, not as a book specifically intended to support someone who is going through a loss or a difficult time. Everyone has their own moment for reading about these subjects.

The book follows a son sitting beside his elderly father as his life draws to a close. As he watches his father grow weaker, he remembers the life they shared: childhood, family stories, the garden his father made, and the things that were left unsaid between them.

The father was a gardener. In the book, the garden is more than a setting. It is connected to his father’s work, to the stories he told, and to the memories his son carries. The publisher’s description brings together images of the childhood strawberry fields, the father’s garden, and the first tulips of spring. These details give the book a sense of a life that continues to matter, even as it approaches its end.

One of the things that stayed with me is the contrast between silence and storytelling. The father is described as a quiet man, but also as someone who knew how to tell stories. That contrast feels familiar to me. In some families, people may not often explain what they feel, but their ways of caring, their habits, and their stories still say a great deal.

I don’t want to reduce the book to a single label. The English publisher calls it a novel, while its description also brings together memory, confession and personal experience. I think it is more useful to say that it is a deeply personal literary work about a father and a son, and about remembering someone while you are still losing them.

Why do I recommend it?

For me, it shows how a very difficult reality can be written about with depth and beauty. The prose feels thoughtful and, at times, poetic. That doesn’t make the pain itself beautiful, and it doesn’t hide what is happening. What I admire is the care with which the book looks at the experience.

It does not offer an easy lesson or a neat answer. It doesn’t tell us that grief follows a simple path, or that a person should be able to turn loss into something positive. Instead, it stays close to the particular details of one family: a father, a son, a garden, old stories, memories of childhood, and the changes that come with the end of a life.

That is what makes the book feel honest to me. It allows the reader to look at something painful through another person’s experience. The story is personal, but reading it can make us think about our own memories: what we remember about the people who raised us, the things they taught us without meaning to, and the moments we understand differently when we look back.

The garden brings those thoughts together. A garden is made over time. Someone plants, tends and waits. The results don’t appear all at once. When I read the book’s central image, I think about the way a person’s life can remain present in what they cared for and in the memories of those who knew them. That is my interpretation of the image, and one of the reasons it has stayed with me.

I would recommend *Death and the Gardener* above all to people who love literature and appreciate writing that is deep, beautiful and sometimes poetic. It may especially appeal to readers who want to look at reality, however difficult, through someone else’s perspective.

I wouldn’t choose it simply because someone is currently grieving or having a hard time. The book deals directly with illness, death and loss. Some readers may want to approach those subjects now; others may prefer to return to them later. The important thing is that each person gets to make that choice for themselves.

So, this week’s book is *Death and the Gardener*, by Georgi Gospodinov: a personal, literary work about a father, a son, memory, silence and a garden. It asks us to stay with a difficult experience and to notice the life that surrounds it.

And it leaves us with the sentence I started with:

“My father was a gardener. Now he is a garden.”

This has been *One Week, One Book*. Thanks for listening.
