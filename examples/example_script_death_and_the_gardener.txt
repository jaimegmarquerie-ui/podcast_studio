Hello and welcome to One Week, One Book, a podcast about choosing a book and taking the time to ask why it might be worth reading. I am very glad you are here with me today.

I want to begin with a line that holds the very heart of the work we are exploring: My father was a gardener. Now he is a garden. It is a simple sentence, but it changes as you sit with it. It first tells us what the father did, and then it turns that fact into an image of how a person can remain in someone else’s memory. It comes from Death and the Gardener, by the Bulgarian writer Georgi Gospodinov.

The first thing to understand about this book is its intimate setting. It follows a son sitting beside his elderly father as life draws to a close, watching him grow weaker. As he sits there, he remembers the life they shared: childhood, family stories, and the things that were left unsaid between them. The central image is the garden itself. A garden is made over time. Someone plants, tends, and waits, and the results do not appear all at once. In this book, the garden shows how a person’s life can remain present in what they cared for, and in the memories of those who knew them.

Along with this, the book grounds memory in the physical world. The garden is far more than a backdrop; it is woven into the father’s work and the stories he told. The memories move through images of childhood strawberry fields, the father’s garden, and the first tulips of spring. These details give a sense of a life that continues to matter, even as it approaches its end.

Another point that stays with the reader is the contrast between silence and storytelling. The father is described as a quiet man, yet also someone who knew how to tell stories. In many families, people may not often explain what they feel in words. Instead, their ways of caring, their everyday habits, and the tales they choose to tell carry meaning and say a great deal.

The book also refuses to offer easy lessons. It blends memory, confession, personal experience, and the qualities of a novel, looking at a difficult reality with depth and poetic prose. It does not hide what is happening, nor does it tell us that grief follows a simple path or that loss must be turned into something positive. Instead, it stays completely honest to the particular details of one family. It invites us to think about our own memories: the people who raised us, the things they taught us without meaning to, and the moments we understand differently when we look back.

Finally, this is a work recommended for its literary depth. It deals directly with illness, death, and grief, so it is not offered as a support book for someone currently moving through a difficult time. Everyone has their own moment for reading about such subjects. It is for those who love literature and want to look at a difficult reality through another person’s perspective.

It asks us to stay with a difficult experience and notice the life that surrounds it. And it leaves us where we began: My father was a gardener. Now he is a garden.
